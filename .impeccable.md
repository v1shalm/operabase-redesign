# Operabase Redesign — Design Context

A Monsoonfish design exercise by Vishal Maurya. Two views of the same `/productions` page wired through a floating Before/After toggle: a faithful clone of today's Operabase, and a proposed redesign that fixes the discovery and engagement gaps documented in the research.

## Design Context

### Users
Three audience segments share one entry point at `/productions`:

- **Casual explorer** — opera enthusiast, no specific show in mind, browsing for "what's on." Short sessions, low patience, no domain expertise to evaluate "what's worth my attention." Currently the platform's biggest drop-off.
- **Location-based planner** — has a trip to Vienna or Milan, wants opera during a specific window. Filters by city and date. Currently frustrated by repetitive listings (same opera × 12 dates as 12 rows).
- **Artist follower** — knows a specific singer or conductor, wants their upcoming dates. Currently has to leave the listing and hunt through artist pages.

90% of all Operabase traffic is general-audience free users. They are the load-bearing wall of the B2B flywheel — if they don't engage, casting directors and opera houses have less reason to subscribe.

### Brand Personality
**Operatic, archival, authoritative.** Operabase has been the global reference for opera since 1996. The brand should feel like a printed program booklet from the Met — serious, considered, slightly old-world — not a startup product. It is a database of cultural record, not an entertainment app.

Three words: **archival, considered, theatrical.**

The redesign keeps that DNA. It's still Operabase. It just gets out of the user's way.

### Aesthetic Direction
**Faithful to current Operabase visual identity.** Same fonts. Same palette. Same spacing economy. The redesign earns its weight through information hierarchy, grouping logic, and progressive disclosure — not through a dark-mode aesthetic flip. The point is to prove that better design doesn't require throwing out brand equity.

**Theme:** Light. Operabase is consumed during planning moments — coffee in hand, deciding what to book — not during performance hours. Light theme matches the editorial/archival feel.

**Reference:** the existing `https://www.operabase.com/productions/en` page itself. The Met Opera's printed program booklet for typographic spirit. Resident Advisor and Bachtrack for handling event listings at scale.

**Anti-references:** dark glassmorphism, Spotify's algorithmic carousel UI, generic SaaS card grids, AI-default cyan-on-dark. The redesign is restrained, paper-weight, archival.

### Typography
Pulled directly from Operabase's stylesheet — no substitutions.

- **Display / Headings:** `Playfair Display`, serif — used for production titles, work names, page titles
- **Body / UI / Labels:** `Roboto` — 300/400/500/700/900 weights — used for everything else
- **Icons:** `Material Symbols Outlined`

**Type scale (matching Operabase's actual scale):** 10 / 11 / 12 / 13 / 14 / 16 / 18 / 20 / 24 / 28 / 32 px. Body baseline is 12px — denser than typical web defaults. Both views must respect this density; opera audiences expect program-booklet information density, not airy SaaS spacing.

### Design Principles

1. **Same skin, better skeleton.** The redesign uses Operabase's exact fonts, colors, and type scale. What changes is information architecture: how performances are grouped, how filters are exposed, what gets surfaced at the listing level vs. behind clicks.

2. **Group by production, not by date.** The single biggest UX failure on the current page is "La Traviata × 12 rows." Each production gets one card that contains its date set. This collapses the listing by 60 to 70 percent without losing any data.

3. **Editorial signals over raw data.** Casual users can't read a row of cast names. They can read a "New Production" badge or a "Conductor's debut" tag. Surface signals at the card level so non-experts have anchors to evaluate "is this worth my attention."

4. **Don't make users leave to learn more.** Quick-look opens in place. The artist preview slides in from the side, without a navigation. The listing remains the spine of the session.

5. **The Before/After toggle is part of the deliverable, not a gimmick.** It's how the work is reviewed. Every screen must answer "what changed and why" by side-by-side comparison. The toggle is persistent (sessionStorage), keyboard-accessible, and sits in a fixed corner that doesn't fight either layout.

6. **Density is a feature, not a bug.** Operabase users come for comprehensive data. The redesign keeps program-booklet density. It does not introduce SaaS-style whitespace inflation.

### Constraints

- **Tech:** Next.js (App Router), TypeScript, CSS Modules. No Tailwind unless forced — Operabase's actual classes are scoped CSS-in-JS, the closest match is CSS Modules.
- **Scope:** `/productions` page only. Header and footer are stubs that visually match Operabase, not full implementations.
- **Data:** mocked in-file. Real Operabase data is fetched client-side from authenticated APIs; we recreate ~12–18 sample productions covering the full range of edge cases (multi-date, single-date, premiere, no-image, sold-out).
- **Deliverable companion:** a `DELIVERABLE.md` write-up covering problem framing, user flows, wireframes (described/linked), rationale, and validation metrics — matching the brief's deliverables checklist.
